Before the 1920s
Films had no fixed speed.
Silent-era cameras were cranked by hand. Most ran somewhere around 16–20 frames a second, and it changed from shot to shot. Projectionists sped films up or slowed them down to suit the show.

Then sound arrived
1927
Talkies needed sound to stay in perfect sync with the picture, and audio only plays back right at one steady speed. So the speed had to be locked.
The industry settled on 24 fps: about the slowest speed that gave decent sound, without burning through expensive film stock.

Your brain does the rest
24 still pictures. One moving world.
Show still images fast enough and your brain stitches them into motion. But 24 flashes a second flickers, so projectors flash every frame 2–3 times: 48 or 72 flashes a second.

The secret ingredient
Motion blur.
The classic 180° shutter rule: at 24 fps, each frame is exposed for 1/48 of a second. Anything moving leaves a soft smear. That blur is a big part of the dreamy “film look”.

Crisp, stuttery-sharp. Feels like live video.

The soap opera effect
Too smooth feels too real.
For decades, news, sport and TV dramas ran at higher motion rates. So our brains link smooth motion with real life, and 24 fps with stories.
With less blur, sets look like sets, makeup looks like makeup, and VFX look like VFX.

And then there’s the bill
More frames, more everything.
Double the frame rate and you roughly double the data, the VFX rendering and the work in post. Shorter exposures also need more light on set.

Where high frame rates win
Slow motion.
120 ÷ 24 = 5×
Shoot at 120 fps, play back at 24, and one second becomes five. Same trick behind every slo-mo hero walk.
